# How We Learn — Notes on Effective Studying

*(Example source, included so the vault and graph aren't empty. Replace with
your own material. This is a short summary of well-established findings in the
science of learning.)*

Decades of cognitive-science research point to a counterintuitive conclusion:
the study methods that *feel* most productive — rereading, highlighting,
cramming — are among the least effective for durable learning. The methods that
feel harder and slower tend to produce far better long-term retention.

Two techniques come up again and again.

**Active recall.** Instead of reviewing material by reading it again, you close
the book and try to retrieve the information from memory — by self-testing,
answering questions, or explaining the idea aloud. The act of struggling to
recall something strengthens the memory far more than passively reviewing it.
This is why practice tests beat rereading, even though rereading feels more
fluent and comfortable.

**Spaced repetition.** Rather than massing all your study into one session
("cramming"), you spread reviews out over increasing intervals — a day later,
then a few days, then a week, then a month. Each review is timed for roughly
when you're about to forget, which makes retrieval effortful and therefore
durable. Flashcard tools like Anki schedule these intervals automatically.

The two ideas reinforce each other: spaced repetition is most powerful when
each spaced review is itself an act of active recall rather than passive
rereading. Together they exploit what researchers call "desirable
difficulties" — the principle that some friction during learning, far from
being a problem, is exactly what makes knowledge stick.
